## 1. What they do

FIDE C.04 defines the **Swiss pairing system** for individual tournaments: a fixed, pre-declared number of rounds; no two players meet twice (C.04.1.b); players are grouped into score brackets round by round and paired against players in or near their own score bracket; colour is balanced (difference of white games minus black games kept within ±2, C.04.1.f) and alternated (no same colour three times running, C.04.1.g); an odd player count produces one pairing-allocated bye per round (full point, no colour, C.04.1.c).

The **Dutch System** (C.04.3.1) is FIDE's default approved Swiss algorithm. It works score-bracket by score-bracket, from the top down: split each bracket into two halves (S1 = higher-ranked half, S2 = lower-ranked half), tentatively pair S1[1]–S2[1], S1[2]–S2[2]… (C.6), then repeatedly **transpose** (reorder S2) and **exchange** (swap players between S1 and S2) until a pairing is found that satisfies as many of the hard and soft criteria as possible (C.1–C.14). Unpairable players are "floated" down to the next bracket. The rules define this as an ordered search: try the strictest requirement first (all pairs formed, all colour preferences honoured, no repeat floats), and mechanically relax one requirement at a time (drop colour preference compliance, drop the same-year-no-repeat-float rule, etc., C.10) until a legal pairing exists.

Annex C.07 defines the **tie-break menu**: an ordered, tournament-specific list of tie-break formulas (Direct Encounter, Buchholz + variants, Sonneborn-Berger, Progressive Score, rating-based measures, etc.) applied in sequence until ties are broken, or — this is the critical fact for our use case — **"any remaining tie should be broken by drawing of lots, unless the rules of the tournament specify that such ties will not be broken"** (C.07 Art. 4.2). FIDE's own official fallback for an unbroken tie is a random draw.

## 2. Why it works

- **Absolute-then-relative criteria hierarchy (C.04.3.1.B)** cleanly separates rules that can never be broken (two players shall not meet twice — B.1; two players who both have an absolute colour preference for the same colour shall not meet — B.2) from rules that are merely optimised for (score-difference minimisation, colour-preference satisfaction, no repeat floats — B.3–B.6). This means the algorithm always terminates in a legal pairing: when a soft criterion can't be satisfied, it is dropped (C.10), never the hard ones.
- **Colour-difference and colour-preference are defined as small integers derived purely from a player's own game history** (C.04.3.1.A.7): colour difference = white games − black games; absolute preference triggers at |difference| > 1 or two same-coloured games in a row. This makes colour allocation a pure function of the player's own signed record, with no external state — directly useful for our "recomputable by a stranger" requirement.
- **The tie-break menu (C.07) explicitly types each formula by its data dependency** (Art. 4.3: Type A = subset of tied players' own games, Type B = participant's own results only, Type C = opponents' final results, Type D = opponents' prior known data such as ratings). This typing is exactly the analysis we need: Type A/B/C tie-breaks are pure functions of the signed game corpus; Type D tie-breaks (Average Rating of Opponents, Performance Rating) additionally require a trusted external rating list, which we do not have for wallet-identified players.
- **Unplayed-round handling (C.07 Art. 15–16)** is precisely specified: a bye or forfeit is scored as a real result for the *opponent's* tie-break calculation (so beating a player who later withdraws still counts at the value awarded, not zero), removing an entire class of exploitable ambiguity.

## 3. What they do badly

- **The rules assume a human Chief Arbiter as the final authority** — "explain the pairing" (C.04.1.i) means explain to a person, not attest cryptographically to a stranger. There is no notion of a signed, independently-recomputable record; FIDE's transparency requirement is satisfied by the arbiter being answerable, not by the algorithm being auditable by an outsider with no special access.
- **The official fallback for an exhausted tie-break list is a random draw** (C.07 Art. 4.2). This is disqualifying for us outright: our own product rules (chit / competition rules) ban chance-based resolution of anything that decides money. We cannot adopt FIDE's tie-break regime unmodified — we must remove "drawing of lots" and replace it with something deterministic.
- **The Dutch algorithm's specification is genuinely hard to implement correctly.** C.1–C.14 plus the transposition/exchange procedures in D.1–D.4 amount to a constrained combinatorial search with several nested fallback loops (C.10's lowering-requirements ladder, C.12's backtracking, C.13's lowest-score-bracket merging). The rules themselves note in D.4 that the "B3-factor" (minimise sum of squared score differences in the lowest bracket) is a special case programmers must handle separately. This is not a format a small team should hand-roll from scratch without a reference implementation to test against (see `swiss.md`).
- **Round 1 colour for board 1 is explicitly decided "by lot"** (C.04.3.1.C.14.2, and the brief examples in C.14.2 of the earlier-numbered text): "The colour to be given to Player #1 is decided by drawing lots." Even the *first move* of a FIDE Swiss tournament has a randomness step baked into the official rules. This is low-stakes (it only decides who is White, not who wins) but it is still a place where the standard, unmodified FIDE procedure is not a pure function of the game corpus — it depends on an external random draw that happened before any games were played.
- **Sonneborn-Berger and Buchholz are both sensitive to opponents' *subsequent* results**, including subsequent withdrawals — C.07's entire Article 16 ("Unplayed Rounds Management") exists specifically to patch the fact that a player who beats someone who later quits gets an unfairly deflated tie-break unless the withdrawal is scored as if the withdrawn player kept their pre-withdrawal score. This is exactly the failure mode Lichess users report in Swiss tie-breaks (see `swiss.md`), and it must be handled explicitly in any implementation we write, not left implicit.

## 4. What we should copy conceptually

- The **absolute-criteria-first, relative-criteria-second hierarchy**: things that must never happen (no repeat pairing) versus things that are merely optimised for (score closeness, colour balance) — this maps directly onto "invariants that must hold" vs. "objective function to minimise" in our own pairing/tie-break code, and it is the right way to reason about correctness.
- **Typing every tie-break by its data dependency** (C.07 Art. 4.3's Type A/B/C/D) before choosing which ones to use. We should do the same triage explicitly and only adopt Type A/B/C tie-breaks (pure functions of the signed game corpus), because Type D requires external, unverifiable rating data we don't have.
- **Unplayed-round scoring rules (C.07 Art. 16)**: treat a bye/forfeit as a real, fixed result for tie-break purposes rather than leaving it undefined — this closes a real correctness gap that FIDE itself had to patch after the fact.
- **The "explainability" requirement (C.04.1.i)** — even though FIDE means it for a human arbiter, the underlying value (anyone should be able to see *why* this pairing/ranking happened) is exactly our own requirement, just enforced by cryptography and open computation instead of by an arbiter's authority.

## 5. What we can do better

- **Never fall back to a random draw.** Where FIDE's C.07 Art. 4.2 says "drawing of lots," we substitute a deterministic, rule-based resolution (see `design.md`): typically an even split of the combined prize across the tied ranks. This is strictly stronger than FIDE's own regulation for our purpose, because our ties decide real money, not just a certificate placing.
- **Replace the round-1 "decided by lot" colour draw with a deterministic seed** derived from public, pre-committed data (e.g. a hash of the sorted list of registered wallet addresses plus the tournament ID). Same output space (fair 50/50 colour split across the field), zero live randomness, and a stranger can recompute it byte-for-byte from data that was public before the tournament started.
- **Make the whole pairing algorithm — not just the standings — a published, deterministic function of the signed record**, and say so explicitly in the product. FIDE's rules only require the arbiter to be able to explain a pairing on request; we can make every pairing independently re-derivable by anyone, which is a strictly higher trust bar and is only possible because we don't need a human arbiter in the loop.

## 6. What is technically required

- A correct implementation of the Dutch System pairing algorithm (C.04.3.1, articles A–E) — see `swiss.md` for the concrete library/engine options (adopt, don't hand-roll).
- A tie-break engine implementing at minimum: Direct Encounter (Art. 6), Sonneborn-Berger (Art. 9.1), Buchholz and Buchholz Cut-1 (Art. 8.1, 14.1.1a), Number of Wins (Art. 7.1), Progressive Score (Art. 7.5) — all Type A/B/C, all pure functions of the signed game corpus (results + rounds + player IDs).
- Unplayed-round handling per Art. 15–16: byes/forfeits recorded with an explicit result value that feeds into opponents' tie-break sums exactly as a played game would.
- A deterministic replacement for every place FIDE's text says "by lot": round-1 colour, and the tie-break-exhausted fallback.
- Signed, timestamped game records (result + round + player identities + signatures) as the single source of truth standings are computed from.

## 7. What could break

- **Misimplementing the Dutch algorithm's backtracking (C.12) or lowest-bracket merging (C.13)** produces pairings that are individually plausible but not reproducible by an independent recomputation — silently violating our own "pure function, stranger-recomputable" requirement without any visible error. This is exactly why we should adopt a tested reference implementation rather than write our own from the prose spec (see `swiss.md`).
- **Forgetting Art. 16's unplayed-round patch** means a beat-then-withdrew opponent silently deflates the winner's Sonneborn-Berger/Buchholz score, producing standings that look wrong to anyone who checks by hand — a real, previously-observed failure mode (see the Lichess Sonneborn-Berger complaints logged in `swiss.md`/`arena.md`).
- **Keeping FIDE's "by lot" fallback for an exhausted tie-break list** is a direct violation of the competition's ban on chance-decided outcomes and must not ship, even as a rare edge case.
- **Small fields make ties more likely, not less** — with 4–16 players (our stated field size), there are far fewer distinct opponents to generate distinguishing Buchholz/SB values, so the "tie-break list exhausted" case is not a hypothetical edge case for us the way it is for a 200-player FIDE Swiss; our deterministic fallback needs to be designed as a normal, expected path, not an afterthought.

## 8. What we can uniquely do because of Nimiq

- FIDE's transparency requirement (C.04.1.i) is satisfied today by trusting a certified human arbiter. We can instead **publish the full signed game-result log and the deterministic pairing/tie-break code**, and settle prize shares on-chain the instant the deterministic standings function finalises — turning "the arbiter can explain it if asked" into "anyone can verify it without asking anyone." NIM's feeless, near-instant transactions mean this settlement can happen automatically, per round or at tournament end, with no manual prize-wiring step — something no OTB FIDE event, and no Lichess free-to-play arena, has any reason to build.
- Wallet addresses double as stable, self-sovereign player identifiers across the whole event, removing the need for a separate participant-registration system that a real FIDE tournament (rating list, federation ID, pairing number per C.04.2.B) requires.

## 9. Licence and reuse verdict

The FIDE Handbook and its annexes (C.04, C.07) are **regulatory text, not software** — there is no licence to "reuse" in a code sense. They are FIDE's official rules, publicly published at https://handbook.fide.com and https://doc.fide.com. We copy *concepts and formulas* from them (which is how every Swiss pairing implementation, including the ones we evaluate in `swiss.md`, describes its own compliance), not source code, and cite the specific article numbers used in our own implementation's documentation. No IP or licence risk in doing so; the risk is purely one of correctness (implementing the specified algorithm faithfully), which is why `swiss.md` recommends adopting a tested engine rather than re-deriving it from this document alone.
