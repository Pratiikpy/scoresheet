# Leela Chess Zero (lc0) — MCTS + neural network search

Research date: 2026-09-08. Sources: https://github.com/LeelaChessZero/lc0 (WebFetch), https://chessprogramming.org/Leela_Chess_Zero and the "Technical Explanation of Leela Chess Zero" wiki/lczero.org pages (via WebSearch), https://lczero.org/dev/wiki/networks/ (WebFetch, checked specifically for network-weight licensing), and cross-reference against `stockfish.md`/`nnue-training.md` for how lc0's approach differs from Stockfish's, as cited inline.

## 1. What they do

- lc0 is "a UCI-compliant chess engine designed to play chess via neural network, specifically those of the LeelaChessZero project" (source: https://github.com/LeelaChessZero/lc0, direct WebFetch of the README).
- **Search algorithm**: Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) using **PUCT** ("Predictor + UCT"), Rosin's UCT improvement — a variant of upper-confidence-bound tree search where a neural network's move-probability output (the *policy*) substitutes for random rollouts to a terminal state (source: WebSearch synthesis of chessprogramming.org's Leela Chess Zero page and the "Alphazero Primer" at https://lczero.org/dev/lc0/search/alphazero/). This is architecturally the opposite of Stockfish's alpha-beta: instead of exhaustively searching and pruning a full-width tree with a cheap-to-evaluate leaf function (NNUE), lc0 runs comparatively few simulations, each one selected by the policy network toward "promising" moves and scored by the value network instead of playing out to a terminal state.
- **Network architecture**: like AlphaZero, lc0's network shares a common "body" that feeds two disjoint "heads" — a **policy head** (a probability distribution over legal moves at that node) and a **value head** (an estimate of the position's outcome) (source: WebSearch synthesis of chessprogramming.org). The network architecture itself has evolved from convolutional (ResNet-style) networks to **transformers since roughly 2022** — one analyzed network is described as having **15 layers and approximately 109 million parameters**, treating each of the 64 board squares as one sequence position (source: WebSearch synthesis citing an academic analysis of Leela's architecture — **the specific 15-layer/109M-parameter figure describes one analyzed network snapshot, not a fixed constant across all lc0 releases, and was not independently re-verified against lc0's own release notes in this pass**).
- **Board representation**: lc0 uses a "color agnostic" representation built from five bitboards (own pieces, opponent pieces, orthogonal sliders, diagonal sliders, and pawns — with en passant target information encoded as pawns placed on rank 1/8), two king-square values, castling rights, and a color-flip flag (source: WebSearch synthesis of the lc0 wiki's "Technical Explanation of Leela Chess Zero" page).
- **Training**: historically self-play, originally sourced from **distributed community compute** contributed by volunteers — structurally the training-side analogue of what fishtest is for Stockfish's testing side, except generating training games rather than validating patches (source: general knowledge of the AlphaZero-derived Leela project lineage, corroborated qualitatively by chessprogramming.org's Leela Chess Zero page framing it as a from-self-play project; **the current, 2026-era state of lc0's training compute model — still community-distributed vs. centralized — was not directly re-verified in this research pass and should be treated as NOT VERIFIED for the present day**).
- Network weight files are downloaded separately at runtime from lc0's own hosting (https://training.lczero.org/networks, per WebSearch) rather than embedded in the engine binary the way Stockfish embeds its `.nnue` file via `incbin/` (contrast with `stockfish.md` §1).

## 2. Why it works

- **The network replaces both search heuristics and hand-written evaluation simultaneously.** Where Stockfish still needs engineered move-ordering (killer/history heuristics, per `stockfish.md` §1) and a per-node evaluation, lc0's policy head *is* the move-ordering signal (which branches get explored at all) and the value head *is* the evaluation, both learned end-to-end from self-play rather than hand-tuned. This lets a comparatively shallow tree — thousands, not millions, of simulated nodes — reach strong play by trusting learned pattern-recognition instead of brute-force depth, and is specifically strong in strategically deep positions where an alpha-beta engine needs much greater search depth to find the right long-term plan.
- **MCTS's simulation count naturally allocates more effort to promising lines** (the PUCT selection formula biases toward high-policy-probability, high-value moves) rather than exploring a full-width tree and pruning — a fundamentally different resource-allocation strategy from alpha-beta's mostly-uniform-then-pruned exploration.

## 3. What they do badly

- **Needs a GPU for competitive speed.** MCTS driven by a large neural network (a 100M-parameter-class transformer, per §1) is comparatively very expensive per simulation next to a bitboard alpha-beta step; on CPU alone, lc0 is much weaker relative to hardware cost than Stockfish's NNUE+alpha-beta combination, which was specifically engineered (per `nnue-training.md` §1–2) to be CPU-fast via sparsity, incremental accumulator updates, and int8/int16 quantization.
- **Reasoning is opaque — arguably more so than NNUE-Stockfish, not less.** NNUE at least produces a single evaluation score per position that a search can reason about via a principal variation; lc0's output during search is closer to "this branch was visited 40% of the simulations" than to a forced-line argument — there is no equivalent of "this refutes that" the way an alpha-beta PV can show. For a product whose stated priority is game-review legibility (the exact axis this project already chose over raw Elo when it rejected akimbo's NNUE — see `nnue-training.md` §3), lc0's search process is a worse fit than either NNUE-Stockfish or a classical alpha-beta engine, not a better one.
- **Training requires enormous self-play compute**, historically sustained by volunteer distributed computing over years (§1) — a resource commitment on the scale of, or larger than, fishtest's testing grid (see `fishtest.md` §3, §7), and one this project has no comparable community for.
- **At very short time controls or low simulation counts, MCTS can be worse than alpha-beta with iterative deepening**, because MCTS needs enough simulations to converge its visit-count distribution toward the truly best move; an alpha-beta search always has *some* principled answer at any depth via iterative deepening, even if shallow (general MCTS-vs-alpha-beta tradeoff, well established in the computer-chess literature; **not independently re-benchmarked in this research pass — treat the general direction as established prior art, the specific magnitude as NOT VERIFIED**).

## 4. What we should copy conceptually

- **Nothing wholesale — the search paradigm itself is the wrong fit for a from-scratch 0x88 alpha-beta engine already built, perft-validated, and working (per project background).** Rebuilding on MCTS+neural-net would discard all of that existing, verified work for a search style that (per §3) is a worse fit for our actual product goal (legible game review), not just a bigger engineering lift.
- Two narrower ideas do transfer, in spirit rather than in code:
  1. **A move-ordering "prior" that biases search toward likely-good moves early** is the same underlying idea as PUCT's policy-guided selection, just engineered rather than learned — which is exactly what our existing killer-move and history-heuristic move ordering already does (per project background). lc0 is confirmation that the *concept* (spend search effort where a prior says it's likely to matter) generalizes across totally different search paradigms; it is not a reason to replace our engineered heuristics with a learned policy network.
  2. **Validate every new network before promoting it**, the same discipline as fishtest applies to code changes and nnue-pytorch applies to trained NNUE nets (see `fishtest.md` §1, `nnue-training.md` §3) — lc0's community has historically run its own play-testing gauntlets before a new network becomes the default download, reinforcing (a third independent instance, alongside Stockfish and nnue-pytorch) that "the loss/self-play-Elo curve looked good" is never treated as sufficient on its own anywhere in this research area.

## 5. What we can do better

- **Because lc0's reasoning is opaque by construction, and our alpha-beta engine's reasoning is inherently inspectable (principal variation, named eval terms, search depth reached), our engine is structurally better suited to game-review than either lc0 or NNUE-Stockfish — not merely "acceptable despite being weaker."** This is the same conclusion `stockfish.md` §5 and `nnue-training.md` §5 reach from the NNUE side; lc0 is a second, independent confirmation from the opposite direction (a totally different search paradigm, same conclusion: opacity is disqualifying for our product regardless of which specific technique caused it).
- **We can treat a strong-but-different-style engine's disagreement with our own engine's move choice as information about *style*, not automatically as proof our engine is wrong** — see §7. Neither Stockfish nor lc0 is "ground truth" in an absolute sense; they're two different strong opinions, and for a teaching product that distinction matters more than it would for a pure-strength benchmark.

## 6. What is technically required

- Nothing here is intended for adoption (§4), so this section records what would be required only for completeness/context: lc0 needs a GPU-accelerated backend (its README and community docs reference CUDA/cuDNN, OpenCL, and other backends per the linking-exception language in its own licence — see §9) — none of which is realistic to run client-side inside a mobile Web Worker/WebView, and running it server-side would face the same "is this actually worth it for our product's axis" question §3/§5 already answer no to.

## 7. What could break

- **N/A for direct adoption**, since §4/§6 rule that out. The one live risk worth naming: if we ever use a strong reference engine (Stockfish and/or lc0) as an oracle to score our own engine's move quality (the same idea proposed for Stockfish in `stockfish.md` §9.1 and `fishtest.md` §6), lc0 specifically carries a **style-mismatch risk that Stockfish doesn't carry to the same degree** — lc0, trained via self-play toward game outcomes rather than tuned against a hand-crafted eval, is known in the broader chess community to favor sharper, more strategically committal long-term positional bets that a shallow alpha-beta engine (or a human) would score as roughly equal or even slightly worse in the short term. Treating a disagreement between our engine's evaluation and lc0's preferred move as automatically "our engine is wrong" would be a mismatch of playing philosophy, not a genuine bug — this needs to be an explicit caveat in any test harness that uses lc0 as a reference, distinct from how a Stockfish disagreement would typically be read.

## 8. What we can uniquely do because of Nimiq

- **Same conclusion as `stockfish.md` §8 and `nnue-training.md` §8, and it applies here for the identical underlying reason: neither lc0 nor Stockfish's ecosystems have ever needed to fund *qualitative* (review-usefulness) labels, because both optimize purely for game-outcome-derived signals (self-play win/loss for lc0's training, SPRT win/draw/loss for Stockfish's testing).** Nimiq's feeless micropayments are the concrete, available mechanism for funding the one kind of dataset this whole research area has never built — a human-judged "was this explanation useful" corpus — and that idea is developed fully in `nnue-training.md` §8 rather than repeated here, since it belongs to the training-data problem, not to lc0's search architecture specifically.
- One lc0-specific angle: if we ever wanted a genuinely orthogonal second opinion for a paid "deep analysis" feature (parallel to the Stockfish-server-side idea in `stockfish.md` §8), lc0's *style* difference (§7) could be framed as a feature rather than a bug for end users — "here's what a search-heavy engine (Stockfish-style) suggests, and here's what a pattern-recognition engine (lc0-style) suggests" as two distinct, monetizable, Nimiq-gated deep-review products rather than one — though this is a speculative product extension, not something evidenced as necessary by this research pass, and should be weighed against added complexity per the organiser's own stated Cycle 2 guidance that complexity costs points (per `SIP_AND_SHIP_C2_CALL1_FINDINGS.md`, referenced in project CLAUDE.md).

The only genuinely safe and useful thing to take from lc0 is the *conceptual* validation that "policy-guided move-ordering beats blind full-width search," which our existing killer/history heuristics already implement, and the observation that all three projects examined in this research pass (Stockfish, nnue-pytorch, lc0) independently converge on "never trust a training/loss metric alone — validate with real games" — reinforcing, a third time over, that our own SPRT-style testing discipline (`fishtest.md`) is the one piece of prior art worth building regardless of engine architecture.
